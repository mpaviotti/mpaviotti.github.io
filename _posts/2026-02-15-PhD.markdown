If you are here you are probably considering getting a PhD in Computer Science or another topic. While a think this is a great idea(!) I would like you to take some time reading this page to make sure you understand the **advantages** as well as the **risks** involved in getting into a PhD program.      

The **TLDR** can probably be summarised as follows: *This is not a degree for the faint-hearted*. 

# 🎓 What a PhD Really Is

A PhD (Doctor of Philosophy) is a research degree focused on creating **new
knowledge**, not just learning existing knowledge.

It is thus radically different from any other university degree.   
As a PhD student you will not necessarily just study books an pass exams, but
focus more on doing independent **original research**, **publishing and
presenting** your findings and finally writing and defending a **doctoral
thesis**.

In other words, you will spend a lot of time reading, writing, creating
knowledge and disseminate it. Essentially, having a PhD demonstrates that you
possess a **deep knowledge** of your own specific field and can engage with
other **international experts** as a peer.

It sounds great, doesn't it? The issue is that you will probably spend more time
failing than succeeding. In all likelihood, you will spend time coming up with
ideas that are wrong, theorems that turn out to be false and you will have to
redo everything all over again.  This may create a breach in self confidence
that makes you doubt whether you are actually cut out for it. Some people do not
overcome this and eventually quit. 

The main issue in my opinion is that a lot of students either **underestimate the amount of work** required, choose the wrong **supervisor** or the wrong **topic**, or do not understand the **requirement for getting a PhD**.   

In some sense a PhD,

*can be the best moment of your life,... or the worst*



## 🔥 I have a PhD in "Making Mistakes" 

One of the most precious life lessons I learned from my PhD is that 

*Failure happens when you try something new* 

If anyone ever mocked or criticised you for having failed at something don't
worry; **Those who are never wrong have never tried anything challenging** and
they just do not understand the valuable lessons you learn when you realised you
have done a mistake.  I personally think I failed in my life more than I
succeeded. In a sense, _I have a PhD in making mistakes_, and I am not ashamed
of saying it.

Failure is just the **the mechanism** with which you learn and doing a PhD
essentially means being  being wrong, uncertain, or behind for long periods of
times; perhaps months, perhaps years. This can be draining, daunting and
dejecting, but doing essentially research means: 

- Submit papers that get rejected ❌  
- Run experiments that fail 💥  
- Write code that doesn’t work 🐛  
- Read things you don’t understand (yet) 🤯  

But, if you can treat failure as data that can be used, confusion as progress,
and rejection as part of life, then a PhD is for you. In other words, if failure
challenges you and drives you forward, and if not knowing things excites you
rather than frustrates you, then this path is for you.

## 🧠 The harsh truth of modern academia  

The harsh truth is that undergraduate degrees are getting easier and easier to
attain, on the other hand, PhDs are **as demanding as ever**. 

When doing an undergraduate degree, you are **safeguarded**; Material is
prepared for you, grades follow standardized metrics, and admission almost
guarantees eventual graduation. A PhD, on the other hand, **does not follow
these rules**; There is **no guarantee of success**.

There is also something to be said about **The AI factor**.  

The AI is essentially a really good *stochastic parrot*.  While it is very good
at solving undergraduate home exercises, it is really bad at doing research.
This is because the AI only regurgitates information that has been already
provided, thus each time you try to push the AI to the boundaries of what is
known it produces garbage. 

This adds another layer of difficulty when transitioning from the B.Sc/M.Sc to a PhD be cause the unscrupulous use of AI has made **undergraduates less trained to work critically**.

# 🔱 The most important component of a PhD

The most important component in your PhD is   

`The relationship with your supervisor`. 

The supervisor is your main collaborator, the person you are going to work with
for a long period of time and whose expertise and personality will strongly
influence your work. 

It is crucial that you find a supervisor whose  **research interests align**
with yours, someone you feel **comfortable working with** and someone whose
mentoring style matches your needs. 

When looking for a PhD, my suggestion is to state upfront what kind of topics
you like and what kind of supervision you want. 

My suggestion is not to negotiate on it because if this relationship fails, it is your PhD that will be in jeopardy.  

On the other hand, while I think the supervisor is responsible for you, there is
nothing a supervisor can do if you decide you do not want to do your part. 

A bad or toxic supervisor can derail even a strong student, while a good supervisor can elevate an average student into an excellent one, but,  
a good supervisor can do little if the student is not willing to pay heed to
feedback and advice or simply not willing to do the work. 

# 🌍 Moving abroad

If you are not a native English speaker, I strongly support the idea of doing a
PhD in an English-speaking country or similar.

For a handful of reasons: 
- these countries are typically the best at supporting research 🔬
- you will hone your English abilities 🇬🇧
- you will learn to interact with a wide variety of different people 🧑‍🤝‍🧑

To get a good PhD you need to be able to collaborate with different people as
good ideas tend to arise in environments with a high level of diversity.   

At the same time, **doing a PhD while living abroad**, confronting yourself with
different cultures and learning a new language  can become **a daunting task**.  
Some people experience significant **cultural shocks** while pursuing their PhD,
which can add up to all the other challenges you will be facing.  

Making sure you like the country you are moving to before accepting a PhD offer
is a good way of mitigating these risks.. even though a lot of people end up
loathing the country they did their PhD in (lmao).


In the end, I can try to warn you about all the risks involved in doing a PhD, but the reality is that **to learn what a PhD is you will have to do one**.
