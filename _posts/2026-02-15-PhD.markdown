If you are here you are probably considering getting a PhD in Computer Science or another topic. While a think this is a great idea(!) I would like you to take some time reading this page to make sure you understand the **advantages** as well as the **risks** involved in getting into a PhD program.      

The **TLDR** can probably be summarised as follows: *This is not a degree for the faint-hearted*. 

# 🎓 What a PhD Really Is

A PhD (Doctor of Philosophy) is a research degree focused on creating **new
knowledge**, not just learning existing knowledge.

It is thus radically different from any other university degree.   
As a PhD student you will not necessarily just study books an pass exams, but
focus more on doing independent **original research**, **publishing and
presenting** your findings and finally writing and defending a **doctoral
thesis**.

In other words, you will spend a lot of time reading, writing, creating
knowledge and disseminate it. Essentially, having a PhD demonstrates that you
possess a **deep knowledge** of your own specific field and can engage with
other **international experts** as a peer.

If it still sounds easy to you here's some facts. A PhD is the **highest
academic degree** awarded by universities (to a first approximation) — held by roughly **2% of the world’s
population** (I took this number from Google, don't ask!). Based on
calculations I've made in a university I worked for, **failure rates for PhD
students can be as high as 40%** .

The reasons why people don't make it to the end of the line can vary from person to person, but common mistakes include **underestimating the amount of work** required, **supervisor mismatch** and/or **topic mismatch**, and not understanding what is the **requirement for getting a PhD**.   

## 🌟 It can be the best moment of your life,... or the worst :pirate_flag: !

A PhD can become, one of your **biggest life achievements** and the most
**intellectually rewarding periods** of your life. This provided that you can
make **consistent progress**, tolerate uncertainty, reach the finish line. 

Otherwise, it can become **a long tunnel with no visible exit**, a slow erosion
of confidence, and a prolonged period of stress and doubt. 

You might think I’m trying to scare you — and to some extent, that’s true — but
it’s better to be honest than to lead you into a trap. 
Uhm, yes, it may become a trap, because once you begin, you’re committing to three or four years of your life, possibly more. 

If halfway through you realize it’s not
right for you, you’re faced with a very difficult decision: acknowledging that you’ve
lost a significant amount of time and quit or keep going for another 
several years and not knowing whether you're going to make it or not.  

That choice can be heartbreaking — for you and for us.

## 🔥 I have a PhD in "Making Mistakes" 

One of the most precious life lessons I learned from my PhD is that 

*Failure happens when you try something new* 

If anyone ever mocked you for having failed at something don't worry; **Those who are never wrong have never tried anything challenging** and they just do not understand the valuable lessons you learn when you realised you have done a mistake. 
I personally think I failed in my life more than I succeeded. In a sense, _I have a PhD in making mistakes_, and I am not ashamed of saying it.

Failure is just the **the mechanism** with which you learn and doing a PhD essentially means being 
being wrong, uncertain, or behind for long periods of times; perhaps months, perhaps years. This can be daunting and dejecting, but doing essentially research means: 

- Submit papers that get rejected ❌  
- Run experiments that fail 💥  
- Write code that doesn’t work 🐛  
- Read things you don’t understand (yet) 🤯  

But, if you can treat failure as data that can be used, confusion as progress, and rejection
as part of life, then a PhD is for you. In other words, if failure challenges
you and drives you forward, and if not knowing things excites you rather than
frustrates you, then this path is for you.

## 🧠 The harsh truth of modern academia  

The harsh truth is that undergraduate degrees are
getting easier and easier to attain, on the other hand, PhDs are **as demanding
as ever**. 

When doing an undergraduate degree, you are **safeguarded**; Material is
prepared for you, grades follow standardized metrics, and admission almost
guarantees eventual graduation. A PhD, on the other hand, **does not follow
these rules**; There is **no guarantee of success**.

There is also an uncomfortable truth about modern academia that we (as a society) will need to address at some point: **The AI factor**.  

Don't get me wrong, it is not a sin to use AI to restructure text, using it as a search engine or as a learning tool, perhaps a learning buddy, but I would
be very careful about using it for doing rigorous work and, even as a learning buddy, sometimes the AI just generates wrong examples, proofs and so on. 

This reminds me a little of the [The bullshit asymmetry principle](https://statmodeling.stat.columbia.edu/2019/01/28/bullshit-asymmetry-principle/), a valuable piece of wisdom I learned as an undergrad. The principle is as follows:  

*The amount of energy needed to refute bullshit is an order of magnitude bigger
than to produce it.*

This makes academics more weary than ever about the papers being submitted and
thus they require solid proofs that your work is correct. In modern academia this is more
true than ever since the unscrupulous use of AI has made undergraduates less
trained to work critically. This is something you are going to need to fix
quickly if you want a PhD.

# 🔱 The most important component of a PhD

The most important component in your PhD is   

`The relationship with your supervisor`. 

The supervisor is your main collaborator, the person you are going to work with for a long period of time and whose expertise and personality will strongly influence your work. 

It is crucial that you find a supervisor whose  **research interests align**
with yours, someone you feel **comfortable working with** and someone whose
mentoring style matches your needs. 

When looking for a PhD, my suggestion is to state upfront what kind of topics
you like and what kind of supervision you want. 

My suggestion is not to negotiate on it because if this relationship fails, it is your PhD that will be in jeopardy.  

On the other hand, while I think the supervisor is responsible for you, there is nothing a supervisor can do if you decide you do not want to do your part. 

A bad or toxic supervisor can derail even a strong student, while a good supervisor can elevate an average student into an excellent one and, but,  
a good supervisor can do little if the students is not willing to pay heed to
feedback and advice or simply not willing to do the work. 

# 🌍 Moving abroad

If you are not a native English speaker, I strongly support the idea of doing a
PhD in an English-speaking country or similar.

For a handful of reasons: 
- these countries are typically the best at supporting research 🔬
- you will hone your English abilities 🇬🇧
- you will learn to interact with a wide variety of different people 🧑‍🤝‍🧑

To get a good PhD you need to be able to collaborate with different
people as good ideas tend to arise in environments with a high level of
diversity. This is, in my opinion, just because we work in a very specialised 
and narrow field and so eventually finding people locally is extremely hard.   
Hence it is extremely important that you learn to communicate and collaborate
with all kind of people regardless of their culture, ethnicity and so on.   

At the same time, **doing a PhD while living abroad**, confronting yourself with
different cultures and learning a new language  can become **a daunting task**.  
Some people experience significant **cultural shocks** while pursuing their PhD,
which can take a heavy toll on their mental health. 

Make sure you like the country you are moving to before accepting a PhD offer.

In terms of funding, often countries like Denmark or the Netherlands have way
more funding for PhD students, they treat students more fairly and ... they pay
more. It is overall a better environment that allows everyone to thrive.
However, the European PhD is typically limited to only 3.5 years so you might
want to consider the USA where PhDs can be as long as 5 to 7 years, so I have
heard. That will allow you to become an expert in your field without worrying
about looking for funding in the near future.

# ✅ Takeaways

Explaining what a PhD is, is not an easy task, it is a challenging and unpredictable journey.  
Everyone’s experience is different, so in the end, **no one can truly tell you
what a PhD is** — you have to experience it yourself, fail, learn, and try
again.

To anyone who is considering doing a PhD, I'd suggest to choose 
- a topic that makes you tick :metal:  
- a supervisor that is willing to supervise you 🤝
- a country that you like 

During times of self-doubt, take care of yourself, don’t beat yourself up and
avoid overindulging in pubs or nightclubs 🍻. Get a good night’s sleep 🛌  and
try again tomorrow. 

(Do as I say, not as I did).
